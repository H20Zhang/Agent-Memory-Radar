# Practice Makes Unsafe: Skill Misevolution in Self-Improving LLM Agents

**arXiv:** [2608.12851](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12851) · **Published:** 2026-08-13 · **Category:** Evaluation & Analysis · **Importance:** 4/5 · **AI confidence:** High

**Tags:** `procedural` `structured` `general-agent` · **Code:** [henrymao2004/misevolve](https://github.com/henrymao2004/misevolve)

> **TL;DR.** Successful but unsafe experience can be generalized into persistent skills, later retrieved in a clean session, and cause fresh harm after the original trigger disappears. Safety therefore has to govern the whole skill-memory lifecycle, not only the source trajectory or final output.

## Problem

Self-improving agents convert successful trajectories into reusable procedures. That makes “success” an insufficient admission criterion: an unsafe action can be abstracted into persistent policy and transferred to later tasks.

Existing safety evaluations tend to measure either current behavior or static artifacts. They do not tell us whether failure occurred at **skill authoring**, **later retrieval**, or **fresh-session execution**. Those distinctions matter because an unsafe artifact does not deterministically cause harm unless the system later selects and follows it.

## Core idea

The paper introduces **SkillMisevo-Gym**, a lifecycle harness that versions persistent skill state, and **SkillMisevo-Bench**, a frozen sequence from malicious exposure to benign and carryover tasks. Nine metrics separately track online behavior, unsafe skill authoring, unsafe retrieval, later attack success, retained utility, and related lifecycle properties.

It also introduces **SafeEvolve**, a wrapper around native skill-evolution methods. A critic inspects each candidate and its lineage; a constrained deleter can remove localized unsafe instructions without adding new workflow. At reuse time, retrieval incorporates lineage risk as well as utility, outcomes are attributed back to reused skills, and risky/low-utility skills can be retired or evicted.

## Memory design

| Stage | Design |
|---|---|
| Write | Native evolution distills successful trajectories into persistent skills; SafeEvolve audits candidate + lineage and applies localized delete-only repair before admission. |
| Organize | Persist versioned skill artifacts with lineage, utility, and risk evidence so descendants remain attributable. |
| Read | Rank reusable skills using utility and lineage risk; separately measure unsafe retrieval and clean-session harm. |
| Update / forget | Attribute later outcomes to retrieved skills; update utility/risk; retire unsafe or low-utility skills and evict by utility-minus-risk. |

## Compared to what

The closest prior warning is **SkillJack**, which shows poisoned experience being transformed into a derived skill that can become harder to inspect and survive source deletion. This paper's distinct contribution is the **evolution lifecycle**: controlled malicious exposure, repeated skill updates, fresh-session carryover, and governance experiments across multiple agent/method configurations.

The SafeEvolve evaluation also compares raw evolution with utility-only and alternative governance conditions, which matters because a safety result without a matched utility-aware control would be difficult to interpret.

## Evidence

Across **25 agent–method configurations**, each with **525 tasks in 25 episodes**, all **21 evolved configurations** author unsafe artifacts and **15** lead to fresh-session harm. That gap is itself informative: unsafe write state and harmful reuse are different failure stages.

In the exposure sweep, only **three malicious tasks** raise carryover ASR from **16.0% to 35.3%**, showing that unsafe procedural memory can accumulate quickly rather than requiring a long poisoning campaign.

Averaged over AutoSkill and EvoSkill, SafeEvolve reduces unsafe-artifact authoring **37.37% → 18.80%**, unsafe retrieval **35.33% → 8.67%**, and carryover ASR **21.33% → 4.00%**. The abstract reports mean benign utility changes by only **0.4 points**. The result should not be simplified to “the defense is free”: utility/safety trade-offs vary by method and metric.

## Why it matters

The important systems implication is **persistent adaptation needs transitive governance**. A safe memory architecture has to observe and control `experience → derived skill → retrieval → execution → outcome → retirement`, because checking only the source record or final output misses intermediate state transitions where risk is generalized and retained.

This also changes how self-improvement should be benchmarked. Aggregate task success can reward precisely the unsafe shortcut that later becomes persistent memory.

## Limitations / questions

- The study centers on executable skill libraries and computer-use/coding workflows, not every form of memory or policy update.
- Benchmark exposures and carryover tasks are controlled; this aids causal attribution but does not establish real production incident rates.
- SafeEvolve's delete-only repair cannot add missing positive checks, allow-lists, or human approval steps.
- Longer natural task streams and multimodal adaptation remain untested.

**Curator take:** **4/5**. Compared with another “agent skills can be unsafe” result, the important addition is lifecycle resolution: authoring, retrieval, and fresh-session harm are separately measurable, and the governance policy is tested at those same boundaries.
